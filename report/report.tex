\begin{abstract}
We study when and why Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) helps Soft
Actor-Critic (SAC) under sparse rewards. We compare four conditions (dense,
dense+HER, sparse, sparse+HER) on the simple \texttt{FetchReach-v3} task,
and focus on the two central sparse-reward conditions on the harder
\texttt{FetchPush-v3} task. On the easy task, all four conditions solve it
near-perfectly; under sparse rewards, HER mainly shortens time-to-competence,
reducing the median steps to 90\% success from 22{,}000 to 8{,}000
($2.75\times$ faster). On the harder task, plain sparse-reward SAC fails to
learn within the $10^6$-step training budget (5.4\% mean final success,
0/10 seeds reaching 90\%), whereas sparse SAC+HER reaches 90.7\% mean final
success (99.0\% median, 9/10 seeds reaching 90\%). This shows a much
stronger benefit of HER on the harder task. Tracking SAC's auto-tuned
entropy coefficient offers a plausible explanation: without HER it
collapses toward zero while the policy is still failing, whereas with HER
it remains substantially higher throughout training. A relabeling-ratio
ablation ($n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} \in \{1,4,8\}$) shows that the benefit
of additional relabeling saturates rather than growing indefinitely.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
In real robotic tasks, the most natural reward specification is often
binary: the task was either accomplished or it was not. Dense reward
functions can provide more feedback, but they usually have to be
hand-engineered, require domain knowledge, and may bias the learned
behavior in unintended ways. However, standard off-policy actor-critic
methods such as Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) can struggle under sparse rewards
because the replay buffer may contain many weakly informative failed
transitions before the agent observes its first success. While Hindsight
Experience Replay (HER) has been shown to improve learning in sparse-reward
settings, it is less clear under which task conditions its benefit is most
significant and what training dynamics accompany that benefit. This report
investigates not only \emph{whether} HER improves SAC, but also
\emph{when} its contribution becomes meaningful as task difficulty
increases and \emph{why} it helps, by analyzing the training dynamics
associated with HER.

\section{Related Work}
This study connects three main topics from the lecture and the RL
literature: off-policy maximum-entropy reinforcement learning,
goal-conditioned reinforcement learning, and reward design under sparse
feedback. SAC~\cite{haarnoja18} serves as the off-policy actor-critic
learning backbone, while HER~\cite{andrychowicz17} provides a
goal-relabeling mechanism that allows failed trajectories to be reused by
replacing the original desired goal with goals that were actually achieved.
The study also relates to Universal Value Function
Approximators~\cite{schaul15} and multi-goal reinforcement learning, since
both the policy and the action-value function must condition on the
desired goal, enabling the same experience to be evaluated with respect to
different goals. The Fetch environments and their sparse and dense reward
variants are based on the multi-goal robotics benchmark introduced by
Plappert et al.~\cite{plappert18} and are available through
Gymnasium-Robotics.

\section{Contribution and Attribution}
\label{sec:contribution}
We build on existing, unmodified implementations rather than reimplementing
published algorithms. SAC and the \texttt{HerReplayBuffer} are provided by
Stable-Baselines3~\cite{raffin21sb3}, while the Fetch environments and
their sparse/dense reward variants are provided by Gymnasium-Robotics,
following the multi-goal benchmark of Plappert et al.~\cite{plappert18}.
The hyperparameters are adopted from the tuned Fetch settings of RL
Baselines3 Zoo~\cite{raffin20zoo}; we did not perform a separate
hyperparameter search. Reimplementing SAC or HER from scratch was
explicitly outside the scope of this study. Our contributions are the
experimental design and the infrastructure required to run, diagnose, and
interpret the experiments: (1) identifying and fixing a reset bug in
\texttt{gymnasium-robotics} 1.3.x that silently made both tasks harder than
documented (\texttt{env\_fix.py}, Section~\ref{sec:setup}); (2) developing
a unified training and evaluation pipeline (\texttt{train.py}) supporting
both tasks, all experimental conditions, and the relabeling-ratio ablation
under one shared configuration; (3) implementing
\texttt{HerDiagnosticsCallback}, which logs the entropy-coefficient and
goal-distance-percentile trajectories used for the training-dynamics
analysis in Section~\ref{sec:mechanism}; this diagnostic is not part of
Stable-Baselines3 and was developed specifically for this study; (4)
designing and executing all 70 main training runs, together with the
separate two-run pilot used to calibrate the FetchPush training budget, and
developing the analysis and plotting pipeline (\texttt{plot\_results.py});
and (5) interpreting the results in Section~\ref{sec:results}, including
the association between HER and the observed entropy dynamics, as well as
the finding that the benefit of increasing the relabeling ratio saturates.

\section{Approach}
We compare four conditions---dense SAC, dense SAC+HER, sparse SAC, and
sparse SAC+HER---on \texttt{FetchReach-v3}, a relatively simple reaching
task. On \texttt{FetchPush-v3}, where the robot must push an object to a
target location and sparse-reward learning is known to be substantially
harder~\cite{andrychowicz17}, we focus on the two central sparse-reward
conditions, sparse SAC and sparse SAC+HER.

Algorithmic hyperparameters are identical across task, reward type, and
HER on/off, so that the reward signal and relabeling strategy remain the
main experimental variables within each comparison. We tested three
hypotheses stated in the project proposal: (i) on the simple reaching task,
HER provides only a modest speedup, since random exploration occasionally
reaches the goal on its own; (ii) on the harder pushing task, HER is
expected to substantially improve learning performance; and (iii) HER's
effect is visible in the training dynamics, e.g.\ in the evolution of
SAC's entropy coefficient and in the distribution of relabeled goals,
potentially reflecting an implicit curriculum.

Section~\ref{sec:results} strongly supports hypotheses (i) and (ii). For
hypothesis (ii), the observed effect was considerably larger than
initially expected: HER made the difference between successful learning
and near-total failure within the training budget. The results provide
partial support for hypothesis (iii), with the entropy-coefficient
dynamics showing the clearest signal and the implicit-curriculum evidence
being weaker.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
    \caption{SAC with optional Hindsight Experience Replay}
    \label{alg:code}
    \begin{algorithmic}
        \Require environment $e$, SAC algorithm $A$, reward function $r$,
                 HER flag $h$, relabels per transition $k$
        \Ensure trained goal-conditioned policy $\pi(a \mid s, g)$
        \State Initialize replay buffer $R$, SAC actor and critic networks
        \While{training budget not reached}
            \State Sample goal $g$ and initial state $s_0$ from $e$
            \State Roll out one episode $(s_0, a_0, \dots, s_T)$ with $\pi(\cdot \mid s, g)$
            \State Store $(s_t, a_t, r(s_t, a_t, g), s_{t+1}, g)$ in $R$ for all $t$
            \If{$h$ is true}
                \For{each $t$ and each of $k$ relabels}
                    \State sample $\tau \sim \mathcal{U}(t+1, T)$;\;
                           $g' \gets$ achieved goal in $s_\tau$
                    \State store $(s_t, a_t, r(s_t, a_t, g'), s_{t+1}, g')$ in $R$
                           \Comment{often a success: $r = 0$}
                \EndFor
            \EndIf
            \State Update SAC actor and critics on minibatches from $R$
        \EndWhile
        \State \Return $\pi$
    \end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:code} illustrates HER conceptually. In our
Stable-Baselines3 implementation, real environment transitions are stored
in the replay buffer, while relabeled virtual transitions are generated
when training minibatches are sampled from \texttt{HerReplayBuffer}. Thus,
the conceptual relabeling shown above is performed during replay sampling
rather than by explicitly storing additional relabeled transitions at
rollout time.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Both tasks use Stable-Baselines3 SAC~\cite{raffin21sb3} with a
\texttt{MultiInputPolicy} for the dictionary observations and share one
fixed hyperparameter configuration adopted from the tuned RL Baselines3
Zoo settings for Fetch-style tasks~\cite{raffin20zoo}. We use
$\gamma = 0.95$, a learning rate of $10^{-3}$, batch size 256, and replay
buffer size $10^6$. For HER runs, we use $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 4$
with the \emph{future} goal-selection strategy unless otherwise stated;
all remaining settings follow the Stable-Baselines3
defaults~\cite{raffin21sb3}. We deliberately did not tune hyperparameters
separately for individual conditions, so that within each task comparison
the reward setting and the use of HER remain the main experimental
variables. The success criterion is the one defined by each environment:
the end effector in FetchReach, or the manipulated object in FetchPush,
must be within 5\,cm of the desired goal.

We additionally found and fixed a reset bug in \texttt{gymnasium-robotics}
1.3.x (the only release line exposing the \texttt{-v3} Fetch environments):
the initial arm pose was not restored on reset, so every episode started
with the gripper approximately 0.55\,m from the goal-sampling center
instead of the documented $\leq$0.26\,m --- a roughly $2.1\times$ larger
initial offset ($0.55/0.26$). The fix (\texttt{env\_fix.py}) backports the
official upstream correction and is applied identically to every run
reported below.

\paragraph{Experimental Scope.} The original proposal specified 5 seeds
per condition and an ablation over $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} \in \{1,4\}$.
We extended the final study to 10 seeds per main condition and added
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 8$ to the ablation in order to obtain a more
robust estimate of the substantial seed-to-seed variability observed on
FetchPush.

On \texttt{FetchReach-v3}, we ran all four conditions---dense SAC, dense
SAC+HER, sparse SAC, and sparse SAC+HER---with 10 seeds each. Each run
used a training budget of 100{,}000 environment steps, with deterministic
evaluation on 20 held-out episodes every 2{,}000 steps.

On \texttt{FetchPush-v3}, we focused on the two central sparse-reward
conditions, sparse SAC and sparse SAC+HER, again using 10 seeds each. A
separate two-run pilot, one seed per condition and 200{,}000 steps per
run, showed that success remained close to zero at that budget (0\% for
sparse SAC and 5\% for sparse SAC+HER), while training throughput was
approximately 54--61 environment steps per second. Based on this pilot,
we used a main training budget of $10^6$ environment steps per run.
Evaluation was performed every 10{,}000 steps rather than every 2{,}000
steps, keeping evaluation overhead proportionate to the approximately
ten-times-longer training budget.

For the HER ablation on \texttt{FetchPush-v3}, we evaluated
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} \in \{1,4,8\}$ using 5 seeds per setting. The
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 4$ condition reuses the first five seeds of
the main sparse SAC+HER experiments rather than repeating those runs. The
final main study therefore consists of 70 training runs, in addition to
the separate two-run pilot.

To support the training-dynamics analysis, the runs additionally logged
SAC's automatically tuned entropy coefficient $\alpha$ and statistics of
$\lVert$achieved goal $-$ desired goal$\rVert$ in replay-buffer
minibatches. These diagnostics were recorded every 2{,}000 steps on
FetchReach and every 10{,}000 steps on FetchPush.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main comparison: does HER's benefit grow with task difficulty?}

Metric definitions used in all tables below: per-seed \emph{success} is the
mean evaluation success rate over the last 5 evaluation points of a run;
\emph{Mean/Median success} aggregate this across seeds within a condition
(we report both because Push has occasional catastrophic-failure seeds
that pull the mean down without affecting the median -- see
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). \emph{Median steps to 90\%} is computed
only over seeds whose evaluation curve reaches 90\% success at some point;
the count of seeds that do so is reported separately in the \emph{Seeds
$\geq$90\%} column, and seeds that never reach it are excluded from the
median (not treated as $\infty$). \emph{AUC} is the evaluation
success-rate curve integrated (trapezoidal rule) over training steps and
normalized by the total training budget, so $\mathrm{AUC}\in[0,1]$.

Table~\ref{tab:reach} confirms hypothesis (i): on the easy task, every
condition eventually succeeds, and HER's main contribution under sparse
rewards is a roughly $2.75\times$ reduction in steps-to-90\% success
(22{,}000 $\to$ 8{,}000), rather than a change in final task solvability.
Table~\ref{tab:push} and Figure~\ref{fig:main}b strongly support
hypothesis (ii): without HER, SAC extracts very little useful learning
signal from the sparse pushing reward within the $10^6$-step training
budget, reaching only 5.4\% mean final success, close to the pre-training
baseline. In contrast, HER on the identical sparse reward signal reaches
90.7\% mean final success (99.0\% median). The gap between mean and
median is caused by a catastrophic-failure seed, discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}. Thus, HER's benefit on FetchPush is not
merely a speedup; within the training budget, it separates near-failure
from successful learning.

\subsection{Mechanism: why does HER help?}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\begin{subfigure}{0.48\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_reach_entropy_coef.pdf}
\caption{FetchReach-v3}
\end{subfigure}
\hfill
\begin{subfigure}{0.48\textwidth}
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4_push_entropy_coef.pdf}
\caption{FetchPush-v3}
\end{subfigure}
\caption{SAC's automatically tuned entropy coefficient $\alpha$ over
training (log scale; mean $\pm$ one standard deviation across seeds). On
FetchReach, $\alpha$ decreases strongly across conditions while the task
is successfully learned. On FetchPush, sparse SAC without HER converges to
a substantially lower entropy coefficient than sparse SAC+HER while
remaining unsuccessful.}
\label{fig:entropy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:entropy} is consistent with a plausible mechanistic
account of hypothesis (ii). On FetchPush, plain sparse-reward SAC receives
very few informative successful transitions and remains near failure
throughout training, reaching only 5.4\% mean final success. At the same
time, SAC's automatically tuned entropy coefficient $\alpha$ decreases to
a very small value, indicating that the policy becomes increasingly
low-entropy despite not having solved the task. This behavior is
consistent with a premature-convergence failure mode.

With HER, relabeled transitions provide substantially more informative
training samples, and $\alpha$ remains markedly higher over much of
training while the policy improves toward high success. This pattern is
consistent with HER being associated with healthier entropy dynamics and
a more stochastic policy during successful learning.

Importantly, entropy reduction itself is not necessarily problematic. On
FetchReach (Figure~\ref{fig:entropy}a), $\alpha$ also decreases strongly
across conditions, but there this reduction coincides with successful
task learning. In other words, entropy reduction tracks genuine
competence on Reach, whereas on FetchPush without HER it occurs while the
policy is still failing. We therefore interpret Figure~\ref{fig:entropy}
as evidence of an association between HER and healthier entropy dynamics,
not as a controlled test establishing entropy as the causal mechanism
behind HER's performance benefit.

For the implicit-curriculum part of hypothesis (iii), the pattern differs
by task. On FetchReach (Figure~\ref{fig:curriculum}a), the median
trained-on goal distance decreases clearly as training progresses, from
approximately 0.10\,m to approximately 0.016--0.020\,m for dense+HER and
sparse+HER, respectively. This pattern is consistent with an implicit
curriculum: as the policy improves, the transitions it is trained on
become associated with progressively smaller goal errors.

On FetchPush (Figure~\ref{fig:curriculum}b), the same effect is much less
visible in the pooled distribution. With $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 4$,
approximately 80\% of sampled training transitions are virtual HER
samples. In our diagnostics, many of these samples have goal distances
close to zero, which keeps both the median and p10 near zero for most of
training. The upper tail of the distribution, captured by p90, is
therefore more informative: it increases from roughly 0.18\,m to a peak
of approximately 0.21--0.215\,m around 150--250k steps and then decreases
toward approximately 0.18\,m by one million steps.

This weaker pattern suggests changing goal-distance statistics during
training, but the pooled metric does not isolate the non-relabeled
transitions and therefore cannot provide strong evidence that an implicit
curriculum is the mechanism responsible for HER's performance gain on
FetchPush. We treat this as a limitation of the diagnostic rather than as
evidence against hypothesis (iii). On the harder task, the
entropy-coefficient signal in Figure~\ref{fig:entropy} provides the
clearer training-dynamics indicator.

\subsection{Ablation: does more relabeling always help?}

The proposal specified an ablation over $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} \in
\{1,4\}$ with 5 seeds per setting. We additionally evaluated
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 8$ using the same number of seeds to test
whether the effect of additional relabeling continues beyond the
originally specified range.

(rows 1--4 are the specified range; row 8 is the extension).}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figures/fig6_push_ablation.pdf}
\caption{Success rate for the HER relabeling-ratio ablation on
FetchPush-v3 (mean $\pm$ one standard deviation across 5 seeds). The wide

\label{fig:ablation}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation} and Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} show that
increasing the relabeling ratio does not produce a clear monotonic or
dramatic improvement in final performance. The setting
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 8$ learns somewhat faster, reaching 90\%
success after a median of 260{,}000 environment steps, compared with
approximately 380{,}000--400{,}000 steps for the smaller ratios. It also
achieves a somewhat higher AUC and mean final success. However,
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 1$ and $4$ show no clear separation with the
available five seeds, and all three settings converge to a similar final
success region by one million environment steps. In particular, the
median final success is 99.0\% for all three relabeling ratios, and 4 of
5 seeds in each setting reach at least 90\% success. Each setting
contains one low-performing seed that fails to reach 90\% success, with
final success between approximately 0.20 and 0.45 for that seed, compared
with at least 0.96 for the other four seeds in the same group. This is
reflected in the wide variability bands in Figure~\ref{fig:ablation} and
indicates that seed-to-seed optimization variability on FetchPush is
substantial relative to the differences caused by the relabeling ratio.
Overall, the ablation suggests diminishing returns from additional
hindsight relabeling. Increasing $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}}$ can improve
sample efficiency, as seen most clearly for $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 8$,
but it does not consistently improve final task performance within the
tested range.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
The central result---HER's benefit growing from a modest speedup on
FetchReach to the difference between near-failure and successful learning
on FetchPush within the training budget---strongly supports hypotheses
(i) and (ii) from the proposal and provides partial support for
hypothesis (iii). The entropy-coefficient trajectories in
Section~\ref{sec:mechanism} offer a plausible mechanistic account of this
difference: HER is associated with avoiding the premature, overconfident
entropy collapse observed for plain sparse-reward SAC when successful
sparse-reward transitions remain very rare.

We note three main limitations. First, the FetchPush experiments show
occasional catastrophic-failure seeds, with final success as low as 0.20
even with HER. With 10 seeds in the main comparison and 5 seeds in the
ablation, such outliers noticeably reduce the mean while leaving the
median largely unchanged. Tables~\ref{tab:push} and~\ref{tab:ablation}
therefore report both mean and median performance.

Second, the goal-distance pattern is consistent with an implicit
curriculum on FetchReach, but is much less informative on FetchPush
because the pooled distribution is dominated by HER-relabeled samples. A
cleaner mechanism analysis on harder tasks would track non-relabeled
("real-goal") transitions separately rather than combining them with
relabeled transitions.

Third, we deviated from the proposal's originally specified evaluation
cadence on FetchPush. Instead of evaluating every 2{,}000 steps, we
evaluated every 10{,}000 steps in order to keep evaluation overhead
proportionate to the approximately ten-times-longer training budget. This
does not change the underlying training procedure or final success
measurements, but it reduces the temporal resolution of metrics such as
steps-to-90\% success and therefore represents a deviation from the
originally specified protocol.

\section{Reproducibility}
\label{sec:repro}
All code, per-run logs, metrics, configuration files, and the LaTeX
source of this report are included in the project repository (see the
link on the title page). Trained model checkpoints are excluded from the
repository because of their size (approximately 540\,MB across the 70
main runs), but are available on request.

The \texttt{README.md} documents the software stack, including the
versions required for the \texttt{gymnasium-robotics} 1.3.x
\texttt{-v3} environments, together with the exact commands needed to
reproduce all experiments. \texttt{EXPERIMENT\_LOG.md} records the
chronological history of experimental decisions, calibration results, and
cluster job IDs underlying the results reported here. The script
\texttt{plot\_results.py} regenerates all figures and summary tables
directly from the stored per-run data.

All 70 main training runs, together with the separate two-run pilot, were
executed on the GWDG/KISSKI HPC cluster. Each run used one NVIDIA A100
GPU, 4 CPU cores, and 16\,GB RAM. The total computational cost was
approximately 115 GPU-hours.

\section{Conclusion}
This report set out to answer not only \emph{whether} HER helps SAC under
sparse rewards, but also \emph{when} its contribution becomes substantial
and \emph{why} it helps. Across the 70 main runs, the results show that
HER's benefit increases markedly from FetchReach to FetchPush. On the
easy reaching task, HER mainly improves sample efficiency under sparse
rewards, reducing the median steps to 90\% success from 22{,}000 to
8{,}000, while all conditions eventually achieve near-perfect performance.
On the harder pushing task, the effect is much larger: within the
one-million-step training budget, sparse SAC remains near failure,
whereas sparse SAC+HER achieves high success in 9 of 10 seeds.

The mechanism analysis provides partial evidence for why this difference
emerges. On FetchPush without HER, SAC's automatically tuned entropy
coefficient decreases to a very small value while the policy is still
failing. With HER, the entropy coefficient remains substantially higher
over much of training while successful learning progresses. This pattern
is consistent with HER being associated with healthier entropy dynamics
and a more stochastic policy during learning, but it does not by itself
establish entropy or exploration as the causal mechanism behind HER's
performance benefit.

The relabeling-ratio ablation further shows that increasing
$n_{\text{sampled\_goal}}$ does not lead to a clear monotonic improvement
in final performance. The setting $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} = 8$ learns
faster and achieves the highest AUC in our experiments, but all tested
values $n_{\text{sampled\_goal}} \in \{1,4,8\}$ reach the same 99\% median
final success. This suggests diminishing returns from additional
hindsight relabeling rather than performance scaling proportionally with
the relabeling ratio.

Natural next steps are: (i) to repeat the mechanism analysis while
tracking non-relabeled transitions separately, which would provide a
cleaner goal-distance signal on FetchPush; (ii) to test whether the
stronger HER benefit observed from FetchReach to FetchPush extends to an
even harder goal-conditioned task such as \texttt{FetchSlide-v3} or
\texttt{FetchPickAndPlace-v3}; and (iii) to quantify uncertainty more
formally using bootstrap confidence intervals or other seed-level
statistical analyses, especially given the catastrophic-failure-seed
variability observed on FetchPush.
